pySCAtter: theory and formulas

This page gives the formulas behind pySCAtter, with the conventions of the CrystalScatter (SasScatter2) C++ code that the program follows. All lengths are in nm and q is in 1/nm. Equation numbers in brackets refer to the papers in the references.

Intensity

Without a lattice, pySCAtter computes the intensity as

\[ I(\mathbf q) = I_0 \left[ \langle P(\mathbf q)\rangle + \beta(q)\,\langle P(\mathbf q)\rangle\,\bigl(S(q) - 1\bigr)\right] + B, \qquad \beta(q) = \frac{\langle F(q)\rangle^2}{\langle P(q)\rangle}, \]

where:

  • P = |F|² is the particle form factor, normalised to P(0) = 1;

  • F is the amplitude;

  • ⟨…⟩ is the average over the size and orientation distributions;

  • S(q) is an optional structure factor (Percus–Yevick, sticky hard spheres or mass fractal), used in the decoupling approximation (Kotlarchyk & Chen 1983);

  • I₀ is the scale (I0) and B the flat background (Base).

With a lattice, the structure factor is replaced by the lattice factor Z(q) of the CrystalScatter form:

\[ I(\mathbf q) = I_0 \left[ S_\mathrm{lat}(\mathbf q)\,\langle P(\mathbf q)\rangle \right] + B, \qquad S_\mathrm{lat} = 1 + \beta(q)\,\bigl(Z(\mathbf q) - 1\bigr)\,G(q), \qquad G(q) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\sigma_\mathrm{DW}^2 q^2\right). \]

G(q) is the Debye–Waller factor. σ_DW (EditDebyeWaller, nm) is the root-mean-square displacement of a particle from its lattice point (Förster 2005, eq. 25; C++ dwfactor = σ²/3). At high q it damps the Bragg peaks and returns the intensity to the diffuse ⟨P⟩. EditBFactor in .ini files is not used by the C++ code, and pySCAtter ignores it too.

Size distribution

All sizes follow a Schulz–Zimm distribution of the reduced size x = R/R̄:

\[ h(x) = \frac{(z+1)^{z+1}}{\Gamma(z+1)}\, x^{z}\, e^{-(z+1)x}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{\sigma_R}{\bar R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{z+1}} . \]

The relative width σ is the parameter EditSigma (or SigmaL for the second length). The averages ⟨Rⁿ⟩ have the closed form ⟨xⁿ⟩ = Γ(z+1+n)/(Γ(z+1)(z+1)ⁿ). This makes the size-averaged form factors expandable as hypergeometric series in q (Wagener & Förster 2023).

Hypergeometric algorithm

Wagener & Förster (2023) write each form factor as a product of generalised hypergeometric functions. The amplitude of a sphere is F = ₀F₁(5/2; −q²R²/4). With the Schulz average, every pixel is evaluated by one of three routes, chosen by its own error estimate:

  1. a power series in (qR)² with the Schulz moments as coefficients, used for small qR;

  2. closed-form asymptotics, which are the large-q expansions of the averaged Bessel products and are exact for σ → 0;

  3. Gauss–Laguerre quadrature over x, for the region in between.

For very narrow distributions (z + s > 140) the Laguerre nodes overflow. A uniform grid of x over ±9 standard deviations is then used instead, and also as a last resort whenever the other routes return a non-finite value.

Cylinders and disks (C++ conventions)

  • Length. The C++ code takes Length as the half-length. This holds for cylinders and for the Length of disks, which is their radius.

  • Two distributions. σ (EditSigma) on the radius and σ_L (SigmaL) on the length are independent Schulz distributions. The orientation-averaged form factor is $\( \langle P\rangle = \int_0^1 d\mu\;\Bigl\langle \bigl[2J_1(qR\sqrt{1-\mu^2})/(qR\sqrt{1-\mu^2})\bigr]^2\Bigr\rangle_R \Bigl\langle \bigl[\sin(qL\mu)/(qL\mu)\bigr]^2\Bigr\rangle_L . \)$ pySCAtter evaluates this exactly (CylinderRL), with each factor computed by the hypergeometric blocks.

  • C++ factorisation. For L/R ≥ 2 the C++ code uses P ≈ P_rod(q)·P_cs(q), and evaluates the cross-section factor at |q| even for oriented cylinders. The isotropic curves differ from the exact average by 1–2 %. In oriented patterns, the streak perpendicular to the axis is much weaker in the C++ code than it physically is. pySCAtter keeps the exact form; see the validation page.

  • Disks. A disk is the same cylinder with the roles exchanged: radius = Length, half-thickness = EditRadius. The C++ disk formula fails (gives wrong values) for Length < EditRadius, which is a rod, not a disk.

Vesicles

The shell amplitude is

\[ F(q) = \frac{R_o^3 F_s(qR_o) - R_i^3 F_s(qR_i)}{R_o^3 - R_i^3}, \qquad F_s(x) = 3\frac{\sin x - x\cos x}{x^3}, \]

with inner radius R_i (EditRadius) and outer radius R_o (Length). The mean radius and the half-thickness carry independent Schulz distributions (σ, σ_L), as in the C++ polyvesicle routine. pySCAtter averages the exact shell amplitude (VesicleRD), while the C++ code uses a thin-shell closed form. The two differ by an overall factor 1.02 and by less than 2 % in shape.

Interiors

  • Core + shell. The amplitude is $\( F = \frac{(\rho_c - \rho_s) V_c F_c(qR_c) + \rho_s V F(qR)}{(\rho_c - \rho_s) V_c + \rho_s V}, \)$ with the core contrast set to 1 and the shell contrast ρ_s (EditRho). This form holds for spheres, cylinders, disks and ellipsoids. For core–shell cylinders the shell ratio is ν = L/R_t.

  • Power-law shell. The shell density is ρ(r) ∝ (r/R_c)^α, built from 20–120 thin homogeneous sub-shells. α = −4/3 describes star polymers in a good solvent (Förster & Burger 1998).

  • Multi-shell / myelin. These use the exact multilayer sum Σ_k Δρ_k V_k F_s(qR_k).

The width w is EditDbeta in degrees; values below 0.5° are taken as 0.5°.

The distributions h(δ) are calculated for particles with one axis: cylinder, disk and ellipsoid, with or without a core + shell interior. The cube is calculated for the isotropic and the perfectly aligned case only. The triaxial ellipsoid, the super ellipsoid and the superball are orientation averaged whatever Ordis is. With a lattice, h(δ) acts on the particle term ⟨P⟩ only; the orientation of the lattice is described under Lattices.

The oriented pattern is $\( \langle P(\mathbf q)\rangle = \frac{\int h(\delta)\,P(\mathbf q, \mathbf n)\, d\Omega_n}{\int h(\delta)\, d\Omega_n}. \)$

Quadrature of the orientation averages

No general spherical grid (Lebedev, Fibonacci, spherical t-design) is used. Every average uses the symmetry of the problem to reduce the dimension, then applies product Gauss–Legendre rules.

  • Isotropic, axisymmetric particles (ellipsoid, cylinder, disk, core–shell, lens, dumbbell): a 1D Gauss–Legendre rule in μ = cos∠(q, n) on [0, 1]. The number of nodes is n = clip(60 + 6 qR_max ν, 80, 1500), at most 400–600 per particle, so the count grows with the number of oscillations in μ. CylinderRL uses n = clip(80 + 8 q max(R, L), 120, 2000). The size averages are done inside the integrand by the hypergeometric blocks.

  • Isotropic, non-axisymmetric particles (triaxial ellipsoid, parallelepiped / cube, superball, super-ellipsoid): a product Gauss–Legendre rule in μ ∈ [0, 1] × φ ∈ [0, π/2]. One octant suffices because of the mirror symmetry.

  • Orientation distributions h(δ) about a director: the averaged particle is axially symmetric about the director, so ⟨P⟩ depends on (q_∥, q_⊥) only. pySCAtter tabulates it on a 56 × 36 grid and interpolates bilinearly to the pixels. At each grid node the average uses:

    • Gauss–Legendre in δ ∈ [0, π] (40 nodes, weight h(δ) sin δ);

    • the midpoint rule in the azimuth ψ (16 points; spectrally accurate for the periodic integrand);

    • 7–21 nodes of the size distribution.

    The fibre pattern is the same table with h = 1 about the beam axis. Perfect orientation (z-dir) needs no average.

  • Lattices: the powder average is analytic in the radial form above, with the azimuthal normalisation N(g) computed by a 200-node Gauss–Legendre rule. The fibre texture of a lattice averages 240 uniform rotations about the fibre axis. An oriented single crystal needs no average.

The moment series of Wagener & Förster (2023, eqs. 13–15) for oriented cylinders is in pysasfit.scatter.orient.pattern_series. The engine does not use it at present.

Frames

In the C++ frame, q_x and q_y lie in the detector plane (horizontal and vertical) and z is along the beam. The director is

\[ \mathbf n_C = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta). \]

The pySCAtter engine uses x along the beam, y horizontal and z vertical, so n = (n_{C,z}, n_{C,x}, n_{C,y}):

  • θ = 0: the axis is along the beam;

  • θ = 90°, φ = 0: the axis is horizontal in the detector plane;

  • θ = 90°, φ = 90°: the axis is vertical. The streak of long cylinders is then horizontal.

The q map of a 2D image is built in one of two ways:

  • Preset mode (Qmax): q_x = q_max·i/N and q_y = q_max·j/N.

  • Detector mode: q is computed from the detector distance, the pixel size and the wavelength. The beam component q_beam = −q²/(2k) comes from the Ewald sphere.

Lattice factor

For every reflection G (Miller indices hkl, multiplicity m, structure factor f), the lattice factor adds a radial peak in |q| − g, with g = |G|, multiplied by an azimuthal factor in the angle ψ between q and G (Förster 2005, 2011; C++ sc_gpu_generic.h):

\[ Z(\mathbf q) = \frac{2\pi}{n V} \sum_{\mathbf G} m\, |f_{hkl}|^2\; s_q\bigl(|\mathbf q| - g\bigr)\; \frac{g_3\, A(\psi)}{N(g)}, \qquad g_3 = \frac{2\pi}{g^2}. \]

  • V is the unit-cell volume (the cell area in 2D) and n the number of particles per cell.

  • A(ψ) is a Gaussian (or the matching peak shape) in the arc length qψ with width φ_w = 4/EditAzi (1/nm), for example A = exp(−4q²ψ²/(π φ_w²)). EditAzi is an azimuthal domain size in nm, the analogue of D for the radial width.

  • N(g) = ∫₀^π A(ψ) sin ψ dψ normalises A over the sphere. It is computed with Gauss–Legendre quadrature.

  • With EditAzi = 0 there is no azimuthal dependence, A = 1 and N = 2, which gives the powder pattern.

  • For the isotropic orientation (Ordis = isotropic) the same holds whatever EditAzi is: the average of A(ψ)/N(g) over all orientations of the crystal is 1/2, so every reflection is spread uniformly over its sphere |q| = g and the 2D pattern shows Debye–Scherrer rings. The 1D curve I(q) is always this powder average.

The factor g₃ = 2π/g² is evaluated at the reflection, not at q (the 1/q² of the textbook powder average). Near a peak the two agree. In the tails, the C++ form keeps the peak symmetric in q.

These are the shapes as the C++ code implements them, constants included:

  • Modified Lorentzians. The denominator uses c₁ but the prefactor uses c₂ or c₃. The peak areas are therefore 0.64 and 0.77 instead of 1, and the widths shrink by the same factors.

Peak width

The width δ is set by two radio buttons, as in the C++ code:

mode

δ

Debye–Scherrer (RadButDebyeScherrer, default)

δ = 4/D

paracrystal (RadioButtonPara)

δ(q) = 4/D + (σ_DW/d_nn)²·d_nn·q²

neither

δ = 1 nm⁻¹ (fixed)

D (EditDomainSize) is the domain size in nm, an absolute length and not a multiple of the unit cell. The relation D = N a = 4/δ is Förster 2011, eqs. 91–92: a domain of N cells has peaks of width ∝ 1/(N a), whatever the lattice. δ is therefore the same for every reflection and every lattice constant. For a Gaussian the FWHM is √(π ln 2)·4/D = 5.90/D. The C++ code does not check D against the lattice constant. A domain smaller than about three unit cells has no physical meaning, and the peaks then merge into a broad hump.

In the paracrystal mode, d_nn is the nearest-neighbour distance:

  • a for sc, lamellae, cylinder packings, hcp and bct;

  • a√2/2 for fcc and Fd3m;

  • a√3/2 for bcc, Ia3d and Im3m.

The width grows as q², so higher orders broaden (Hosemann’s paracrystal, cumulative disorder). σ_DW (EditDebyeWaller) sets both this width and the Debye–Waller factor.

Lattices

pySCAtter generates the reflections from the lattice type, its cell parameters and hkl up to HKLmax. It applies the structure factor of the motif and the extinction rules of CrystalScatter:

  • sc, bcc, fcc, hcp, bct, Fd3m and orthorhombic;

  • lamellae and the 2D cylinder packings (hexagonal, square, centred rectangular);

  • gyroid (Ia3d), OBDD (Pn3m) and Plumber’s Nightmare (Im3m), with the reflection conditions of the bicontinuous phases;

  • the GISAXS entries, calculated as the same lattices in transmission.

Each lattice type takes only the cell parameters that define it:

The cell is built in its own frame with a along x, b in the x–y plane and c completing it; without a rotation this frame is the laboratory frame (x along the beam, y horizontal, z vertical).

Orientation. The cell is oriented by a zone axis [uvw] and a rotation ψ about it (Förster 2011, section 3). The real-space direction v = ua + vb + wc is turned onto the beam by the rotation about v × x̂ through the angle between them; the crystal is then rotated by ψ about the beam:

\[ \mathsf R = \mathsf R_{\hat{\mathbf x}}(\psi)\;\mathsf R_{\mathbf v\times\hat{\mathbf x}}\bigl(\angle(\mathbf v, \hat{\mathbf x})\bigr), \qquad \mathbf G_\mathrm{lab} = \mathsf R\,\mathbf G . \]

The same rotation applies to the 1D and 2D lattices, for which b and c (lamellae) or c (rods) are the non-periodic directions:

  • cylinder packings with the zone axis [001] have the rods along the beam, and the reflections hk0 lie in the detector plane; with [100] and ψ = 0 the rods are vertical;

  • lamellae with the zone axis [010] (layer normal horizontal at ψ = 0) or [001] (vertical) are edge-on, and the reflections h00 lie in the detector plane, turned by ψ; with [100] the layer normal is along the beam and no reflection reaches the detector.

For the isotropic orientation the rotation drops out, and for the fibre pattern the lattice is averaged over 240 rotations about the beam after it. The fibre average uses point-like 3D peaks; the modified Lorentzian II (exponent 3/2) cannot be normalised in 3D and is not available there.

Fitting

The fit minimises

\[ \chi^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{I_\mathrm{model}(q_i) - I_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 \]

by trust-region-reflective least squares, within the parameter ranges of the SasScatter GUI.

  • In 1D the model is folded with the Gaussian q resolution σ_q (Qdev) by 15-point Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

  • In 2D the model is evaluated at the q vector of every used pixel.

  • The standard errors are √diag((JᵀJ)⁻¹ χ²ᵣ).

  • The optional relative residuals use ln I·|I|/σ in place of I.
