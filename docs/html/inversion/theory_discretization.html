Discretization of the integral

General scheme

Every discretization represents the unknown by a finite expansion

(1)\[x(r) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} c_k\, \varphi_k(r)\]

and turns (1) into the linear system \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}\approx\mathbf{b}\) with

(2)\[A_{ik} = \int_0^{r_{max}} K(q_i,r)\,\varphi_k(r)\,\mathrm{d}r \qquad\text{(collocation in } q\text{)} .\]

The data are matched exactly at the measured \(q_i\) (collocation); Galerkin projections are not used because the data exist only at discrete \(q\). Three choices of \(\varphi_k\) are implemented: point values with a simple quadrature, point values with interpolation and a fine quadrature, and cubic B-splines. Direct collocation on a point grid

pysasfit.inversion.kernels.build_size_distribution_kernel() uses the grid points themselves as quadrature nodes,

(3)\[A_{ij} = \Delta r_j\; r_j^{-\alpha}\, K(q_i, r_j),\qquad \Delta r_j = \tfrac12 (r_{j+1}-r_{j-1}),\]

(numpy.gradient; one-sided at the ends). This is the rectangle/ trapezoid rule and is accurate only if the kernel varies little between neighbouring points. For the \(j_0\) kernel the fastest oscillation in \(r\) has period \(2\pi/q_{max}\), so a grid step \(\Delta r>\pi/q_{max}\) undersamples the kernel at high \(q\) and no coefficient vector can fit those data. On test.dat (\(\pi/q_{max}=8.2\) nm, \(D_{max}=782\) nm) even unregularized least squares gives \(\chi^2_r = 460\)–\(840\) for \(N\le45\) points.

Point values with interpolation and fine quadrature

To decouple the number of unknowns from the quadrature accuracy, build_kernel_on_nodes() keeps the node values \(c_k=x(r_k)\) as unknowns, interpolates between the nodes with fixed cardinal functions \(\varphi_k\) (\(\varphi_k(r_j)=\delta_{jk}\)), and evaluates (2) on a fine uniform grid \(\rho_l\):

(4)\[\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^{fine}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},\qquad A^{fine}_{il} = \Delta\rho\, \rho_l^{-\alpha} K(q_i,\rho_l),\qquad \Phi_{lk} = \varphi_k(\rho_l).\]

The size of the fine grid (quadrature_points()) is

(5)\[n_{q} = \max\!\left(4(N-1)+1,\; \left\lceil \frac{4\, r_{max}\, q_{max}}{\pi}\right\rceil + 1\right),\]

i.e. at least four points per Shannon interval \(\pi/q_{max}\) and at least four per node interval. The node kernel reproduces a reference integral of a sphere PDDF to \(2.7\times10^{-4}\) (relative) on a 60-point quadratic grid.

Linear interpolation (hat functions)
\[\begin{split}\varphi_k(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{r-r_{k-1}}{r_k-r_{k-1}} & r_{k-1}\le r\le r_k\\[1ex] \dfrac{r_{k+1}-r}{r_{k+1}-r_k} & r_k\le r\le r_{k+1}\\[1ex] 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}\]

\(\varphi_k\ge 0\) and \(\sum_k\varphi_k=1\): non-negative node values give a non-negative \(x(r)\) and a non-negative kernel stays non-negative. Hence linear interpolation is compatible with every solver.

Cubic spline interpolation (not-a-knot)

\(\varphi_k\) is the \(C^2\) cubic interpolating spline through the unit vector \(\mathbf{e}_k\) (cubic_basis()). On each interval \(x(r)\) is a cubic; \(x\), \(x'\) and \(x''\) are continuous at the inner nodes. The two missing conditions are the not-a-knot conditions – \(x'''\) is also continuous at \(r_2\) and \(r_{N-1}\), i.e. the first two and the last two intervals are each one cubic. The alternative natural condition \(x''(0)=x''(r_{max})=0\) is wrong for a PDDF, which behaves like \(r^2\) near the origin (test.dat, uniform 45 points: \(\chi^2_r\) 2.42 natural vs 0.65 not-a-knot). The interpolation is exact for cubic polynomials and \(\sum_k\varphi_k = 1\).

The cardinal cubic splines oscillate: they have negative lobes (minimum about \(-3.2\) on a 60-point log grid, where adjacent intervals differ strongly in length). Consequently non-negative node values do not guarantee \(x(r)\ge0\), and a non-negative kernel becomes slightly signed (3 % negative entries for the sphere kernel). The GUI therefore uses linear interpolation for the solvers that need \(x\ge0\) or a non-negative kernel (EM + smoothing, EM + MaxEnt, Hansen MaxEnt, ARLS \(\ge0\)).

Spacing of the grid points

make_r_grid() places \(N\) points on \([0,r_{max}]\) with \(u_k = (k-1)/(N-1)\):

\[\text{uniform: } r_k = r_{max}\,u_k ,\qquad \text{quadratic: } r_k = r_{max}\,u_k^2 ,\]
\[\text{log: } r_1 = 0,\quad r_k = r_{max}\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)^{\frac{N-k}{N-2}},\ k=2\dots N ,\]

(a geometric series from \(r_{max}/(2N)\) to \(r_{max}\)). Dense spacing at small \(r\) suits a PDDF that rises steeply and has a long smooth tail; size distributions with structure at large \(r\) prefer the uniform grid.

All smoothness penalties of the next chapter are built from differences of neighbouring unknowns. On a non-uniform grid they therefore measure smoothness in the grid index – on a log grid, smoothness in \(\ln r\), which gives the solution more freedom at small \(r\).

Cubic B-spline basis

Following Glatter (1977) the unknown can be represented by \(N_c\ll N_s\) cubic B-splines (pysasfit.inversion.spline_basis):

(6)\[x(r) = \sum_{k=1}^{N_c} c_k\, B_{k}(r/r_{max}) .\]
Knot vector

Clamped, on \(t=r/r_{max}\in[0,1]\), degree \(d=3\):

\[\boldsymbol\tau = (\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{d+1},\ \tau_1,\dots,\tau_{K},\ \underbrace{1,1,1,1}_{d+1}),\qquad K = N_c + 2 - d - 1 .\]

The interior knots follow the same three rules as the grid points (interior_knots()): uniform \(\tau_j = j/(K+1)\), quadratic \(\tau_j=(j/(K+1))^2\), log geometric from \(1/(2N_c)\) to 1.

Cox–de Boor recursion
\[\begin{split}B_{j,0}(t) = \begin{cases}1 & \tau_j\le t<\tau_{j+1}\\ 0 & \text{else}\end{cases}, \qquad B_{j,d}(t) = \frac{t-\tau_j}{\tau_{j+d}-\tau_j}B_{j,d-1}(t) + \frac{\tau_{j+d+1}-t}{\tau_{j+d+1}-\tau_{j+1}}B_{j+1,d-1}(t).\end{split}\]

The clamped basis has \(N_c+2\) functions; the first and the last (the only ones that are non-zero at \(t=0\) and \(t=1\)) are dropped, which builds \(x(0)=x(r_{max})=0\) into the basis instead of imposing it by a penalty.

Properties

\(B_k\ge0\), local support on four knot intervals, \(C^2\) continuity. By the convex-hull property, \(\mathbf c\ge0\) implies \(x(r)\ge0\); a non-negative kernel stays non-negative. Every solver – including the EM and MaxEnt solvers – can therefore be used unchanged on the coefficients.

Kernel and results

With \(\boldsymbol\Phi_{lk}=B_k(\rho_l/r_{max})\) on the fine grid (5) and \(\mathbf{B}_{jk} = B_k(r_j/r_{max})\) on the display grid,

\[\mathbf{A}_{spline} = \mathbf{A}^{fine}\boldsymbol\Phi \in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_c},\qquad \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{c},\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{c})\,\mathbf{B}^T .\]

pysasfit.inversion.solver_registry.run_solver() with spline=SplineOptions(n_coeffs, spacing, A_quad) runs any registered solver on \(\mathbf{A}_{spline}\) and maps the result back. The solvers’ smoothness operators then act on neighbouring coefficients.

Why it helps

A cubic B-spline basis with knot spacing \(\Delta\tau\,r_{max}\) cannot represent oscillations shorter than about \(2\Delta\tau\,r_{max}\). With \(N_c=20\) the basis cannot follow individual high-\(q\) points of log-spaced data, while a 95-point grid can. On test.dat (\(j_0\), 20 coefficients, log knots) all Bayesian-evidence and Tikhonov variants reach \(\chi^2_r\approx1\) with a high-\(q\) \(\chi^2\) of about 1.05, against 0.02–0.1 on the 95-point grid, and the Bayesian normalized evidence increases from \(\ln Z=-153.0\) to \(-118.8\). With the sphere kernel, the spurious spike at \(r\approx3\) nm of the volume-weighted point-grid solution disappears.

The number of coefficients is the regularization knob of this representation (typically 15–30); knot spacing matters as well (uniform knots needed about 25 coefficients on test.dat, log/quadratic knots 20).

Alternatives in the literature

  • Sine series (Moore 1980): \(p(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \sin(n\pi r/D_{max})\). The basis functions are the Shannon-optimal functions for a PDDF, vanish at both ends, and the integrals (2) with \(j_0\) have closed forms. It is linear and signed (no positivity). Not implemented.

  • Shape-preserving cubic interpolation (PCHIP, Fritsch & Carlson 1980) does not overshoot, but its slopes depend non-linearly on the node values, so it cannot be written as a fixed matrix \(\boldsymbol\Phi\); not usable in the linear framework.

  • Higher-order quadrature (Simpson, Gauss) of the collocation integrals improves the accuracy of (3) but not the undersampling problem, which is solved by the fine quadrature above.
