Forward model, data and information content

The Fredholm equation

All inversions in this package solve a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind

(1)\[I(q) - I_{bg} \;=\; \int_0^{r_{max}} K(q,r)\, x(r)\, \mathrm{d}r ,\]

for the unknown function \(x(r)\), given \(M\) noisy samples \(b_i = I(q_i)-I_{bg}\) with uncertainties \(\sigma_i\). The kernel \(K\) is smooth in \(r\), so the integral operator damps high-frequency components of \(x\) strongly; inverting it amplifies noise without bound. The problem is ill-posed and every method below is a way of adding prior information (smoothness, positivity, entropy, a reduced basis) to obtain a stable, physically meaningful solution.

Kernels

Four kernels are registered in pysasfit.inversion.kernel_registry.KERNEL_REGISTRY: three pair-distance kernels for particles of spherical, cylindrical and lamellar symmetry (Hansen 2016, eqs. 1-3) and one size-distribution kernel.

Pair-distance distribution (sinc_4pi)

For a particle with excess scattering length density \(\Delta\rho(\mathbf r)\) the orientationally averaged intensity is the Debye/Fourier-sine relation

(2)\[I(q) = 4\pi \int_0^{D_{max}} p(r)\, j_0(qr)\, \mathrm{d}r , \qquad j_0(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x},\]

with \(p(r) = r^2\,\tilde\gamma(r)\), \(\tilde\gamma\) the autocorrelation of \(\Delta\rho\). Hence \(K(q,r)=4\pi j_0(qr)\); the factor \(r^2\) is part of the unknown \(p(r)\). Properties used throughout:

  • \(p(0)=0\) (it vanishes like \(r^2\)) and \(p(r)=0\) for \(r \ge D_{max}\); the \(r\) grid therefore always starts at \(r=0\).

  • The kernel is signed: \(j_0(qr)<0\) for \(\pi<qr<2\pi\), etc. Methods that require a non-negative kernel (plain EM, EM + MaxEnt) are not applicable.

  • \(p(r)\) may itself be negative for particles with an internal contrast that changes sign (core–shell, contrast variation). No positivity is imposed by default.

  • There is no size weighting (uses_alpha=False).

Cross-section distribution of a long cylinder (cylinder_pddf)

For a rod of length \(L \gg 2\pi/q\) the orientational average factorizes into \(\pi/(qL)\) times the scattering of the cross-section (Glatter 1979; Hansen 2016, eq. 2). Per unit length

(3)\[I(q)\,\big/\,L = \frac{2\pi^2}{q} \int_0^{D_{max}} p_c(r)\, J_0(qr)\, \mathrm{d}r ,\]

with \(p_c(r) = r\,\gamma_c(r)\), \(\gamma_c\) the 2D autocorrelation of the cross-section density and \(J_0\) the cylindrical Bessel function. \(D_{max}\) is the largest distance within the cross-section (its diameter); \(p_c(0)=0\). As for the sphere the kernel is signed and \(p_c\) may be negative. The kernel diverges like \(1/q\); the forward value is that of the reduced intensity, \(\lim_{q\to0} q\,I(q) = 2\pi^2\int p_c\,\mathrm{d}r\).

Thickness distribution of a flat lamella (lamella_pddf)

For a sheet of area \(A \gg (2\pi/q)^2\) the Lorentz factor is \(2\pi/(q^2 A)\) (Hansen 2016, eq. 3). Per unit area

(4)\[I(q)\,\big/\,A = \frac{4\pi}{q^2} \int_0^{D_{max}} p_t(r)\, \cos(qr)\, \mathrm{d}r ,\]

with \(p_t(r)=\gamma_t(r)=\int\Delta\rho(x)\,\Delta\rho(x+r)\,\mathrm{d}x\) the 1D autocorrelation of the profile across the sheet and \(D_{max}\) the thickness. Unlike the other two, \(p_t(0)=\int\Delta\rho^2\,\mathrm{d}x>0\), and \(p_t\) is the restriction to \(r\ge0\) of an even function, so \(p_t'(0)=0\). A smoothness prior that pulls \(p(0)\) to zero (Hansen 2000, eq. 19) is therefore wrong here; the variant hansen_eq19_even (see Regularization and choice of the regularization strength) leaves \(p(0)\) free and is selected automatically in the GUI. In a test with a core–shell sheet (tests/inversion/test_cylinder_lamella_kernels.py) the relative error of \(p_t\) is 3 % with the free end and 37 % with \(p(0)\) pinned, and the evidence prefers the free end by \(\Delta\ln Z\approx 8.5\).

General relation (Hansen 2016, eqs. 4-5): \(p(r)=r^{d-1}\gamma(r)\) with \(d=3,2,1\) for sphere, cylinder and lamella, and

\[\lim_{q\to0} q^{3-d}\,I(q) = c_d \int_0^{D_{max}} p(r)\,\mathrm{d}r, \qquad c_3 = 4\pi,\ c_2 = 2\pi^2,\ c_1 = 4\pi ,\]
\[R_{g,d}^2 = \frac{\int p(r)\, r^2\, \mathrm{d}r}{2\int p(r)\, \mathrm{d}r},\]

the radius of gyration of the particle (\(R_g\)), of the cross-section (\(R_c\)) or of the thickness (\(R_t\)). The GUI reports these, plus \(R=\sqrt2\,R_c\) (homogeneous circular cross-section) and \(T=\sqrt{12}\,R_t\) (homogeneous sheet).

The normalization is the same as in pysasfit.sldprofile: a \(p(r)\) computed with pysasfit.sldprofile.geometry.pddf() from a radial density profile reproduces the exact intensity of that profile with these kernels (checked in the test above). The density profile \(\Delta\rho(r)\) itself – Hansen’s direct estimate or Glatter’s two-step deconvolution of \(p(r)\) – is computed by pysasfit.sldprofile (SLD profile).

Sphere size distribution (sphere_rg)

For a dilute polydisperse system of homogeneous spheres of radius \(R\) with number distribution \(N(R)\)

(5)\[I(q) = \int_0^{\infty} N(R)\, R^6\, \left[ \frac{3\left(\sin qR - qR\cos qR\right)}{(qR)^3}\right]^2 \mathrm{d}R ,\]

up to the constant \((4\pi/3)^2\Delta\rho^2\). The factor \(R^6\propto V^2\) must be kept: it sets the relative weight of small and large particles. The kernel is non-negative. The variable \(r\) of the grid is the radius, so the suggested \(r_{max}\) is \(D_{max}/2\).

Size weighting

Following Kohlbrecher & Bressler (2022, eq. 32–34) the unknown is rescaled as

(6)\[x(R) = N(R)\,R^{\alpha}, \qquad K_\alpha(q,R) = R^{-\alpha} K(q,R),\]

which leaves (1) unchanged. \(\alpha=0, 3, 6\) give number, volume and volume-squared (intensity) weighted distributions. For the sphere kernel \(\alpha=6\) divides the \(R^6\) out of the kernel; with \(\alpha=0\) the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) span about 15 decades and the EM iteration fails. At \(R=0\) the column is set to zero for \(\alpha>0\). pysasfit.inversion.kernels.recover_N_from_x() undoes the substitution.

Background

pysasfit.inversion.background.fit_background() fits

\[I(q) \approx I_{bg} + c\,q^{-\alpha_{bg}}\]

by weighted non-linear least squares in a chosen \(q\) range. Only the constant \(I_{bg}\) is subtracted (BackgroundFitResult.evaluate deliberately evaluates the power law with \(c=0\)); the power law only serves to separate the flat background from the decaying particle scattering in the fit. extrapolation_risk measures in units of the fit range’s own log width how far the data extend outside the fit range, because a power law extrapolated by a decade can exceed the signal. A fixed, user-given constant can be used instead.

Information content and the choice of the grid

Maximum dimension

Glatter’s consistency condition \(q_{min}\le\pi/D_{max}\) gives the largest dimension the low-\(q\) end of the data can constrain. The GUI suggests

\[\begin{split}D_{max} \approx \frac{\pi}{q_{min}},\qquad r_{max} = \begin{cases} D_{max} & \text{PDDF}\\ D_{max}/2 & \text{sphere radius}\end{cases}\end{split}\]

For tests/data/test.dat (\(q_{min}=0.004016\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\)) this is \(D_{max}\approx 782\) nm. Larger values let the solver “improve” \(\chi^2\) by fitting noise at mid \(q\).

Shannon sampling

\(p(r)\) on \([0,D_{max}]\) and \(I(q)\) are a Fourier pair. The sampling theorem says that \(I(q)\) is fully determined by its values at the Shannon points \(q_k = k\pi/D_{max}\), and \(p(r)\) measured up to \(q_{max}\) is resolved on a length scale

\[\Delta r_{Shannon} = \frac{\pi}{q_{max}} .\]

The number of independent parameters (Shannon channels) in the data is

(7)\[N_s = \frac{(q_{max}-q_{min})\,D_{max}}{\pi},\]

which the GUI suggests as the number of \(r\) grid points (test.dat: \(\pi/q_{max}=8.2\) nm, \(N_s\approx 95\) for the PDDF, 47 for the sphere radius).

Log-spaced data

Most SAS data are equidistant in \(\log q\). The number of data points per Shannon channel is then

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}k} = \frac{\pi}{D_{max}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}q} = \frac{\pi}{D_{max}}\,\frac{1}{q\,\Delta\ln q},\]

i.e. it decreases like \(1/q\). On test.dat it drops to about 0.45 points per channel at high \(q\). A model with one free parameter per channel (a point grid with \(N\approx N_s\)) can then follow every single high-\(q\) point, including its noise – this was the “high-\(q\) overfitting” analysed in Development history and findings, and it is the main reason for the reduced B-spline basis (Discretization of the integral).

Goodness of fit

\[\chi^2_r = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \frac{\left[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i - b_i\right]^2}{\sigma_i^2}\]

is used as the fit criterion (target value 1 in the discrepancy principle). For diagnosing where a fit is too good or too bad, the residuals are split into the lower, middle and upper third of the \(q\) points and the mean of \([(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i-b_i]^2/\sigma_i^2\) is reported per third (“low/mid/high-\(q\) \(\chi^2\)”); a value far below 1 in one region means that the model follows the noise there. Data without error bars ———————–

Every solver needs \(\sigma_i\) (weights, \(\chi^2_r\), discrepancy principle). For files without a \(\Delta I\) column – or to check the given one – pysasfit.inversion.goodness_of_fit.estimate_errors() provides three error models.

Local cubic fits (default without \(\Delta I\))

For every point a cubic in \(\ln I\) versus \(\ln q\) is fitted by least squares to the point and its two neighbours on each side (local_polynomial_scatter()). With \(n=5\) points and \(p=4\) parameters the residual sum of squares has \(\nu=n-p=1\) degree of freedom, and

\[s_i^2 = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}_i}{\nu} = \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_{k=i-2}^{i+2} \left[\ln I_k - P_i(\ln q_k)\right]^2\]

is an unbiased estimate of the relative variance \(\varepsilon_i^2\). (The plain mean square deviation \(\mathrm{RSS}/n\) is too small by \(\nu/n=1/5\).) The cubic removes the local shape of the curve up to third order. For equidistant points the single residual degree of freedom is proportional to the fourth difference, \(s_i\propto|y_{i-2}-4y_{i-1}+6y_i-4y_{i+1}+y_{i+2}|/\sqrt{70}\).

A single \(s_i\) is \(|z|\) of one Gaussian variable (\(s_i^2\sim\varepsilon^2\chi^2_1\)): unbiased in the variance, but its 5–95 % range is \(0.06\)–\(2\,\varepsilon\), and weights \(1/\sigma_i^2\) built from it can be wrong by orders of magnitude. The estimates are therefore combined over a window of \(N\) points (default 15) by a running median, scaled to be unbiased for Gaussian noise,

\[\hat\varepsilon_i = \frac{\mathrm{median}_{j\in\mathcal W_i}\, s_j} {\sqrt{\mathrm{median}(\chi^2_\nu)/\nu}} \quad(=1.4826\,\mathrm{median}\,s_j \text{ for } \nu=1),\qquad \sigma_i = \hat\varepsilon_i\,|I_i| .\]

\(N=1\) is the plain per-point estimate. Near the ends the first or last five points are used.

Neighbour interpolation

Each point is compared with the line through its two neighbours in \(\ln I\) versus \(\ln q\),

\[d_i = \ln I_i - \left[w_i\ln I_{i-1} + (1-w_i)\ln I_{i+1}\right],\qquad w_i = \frac{\ln q_{i+1}-\ln q_i}{\ln q_{i+1}-\ln q_{i-1}},\]

\(\mathrm{Var}(d_i)=\varepsilon^2(1+w_i^2+(1-w_i)^2)\), combined the same way: \(\hat\varepsilon_i = 1.4826\,\mathrm{median}_{\mathcal W_i}|d_j|/\sqrt{1+w_j^2+(1-w_j)^2}\). Only linear trends are removed, so curvature (steep slopes, form-factor minima) inflates the estimate.

Poisson

\(\sigma_i^2 = I_i\), valid only for intensities in counts.

Relative error

\(\sigma_i = r\,|I_i|\) with a given \(r\).

If the shape of \(\sigma_i\) is right but its overall level is not (estimated or guessed errors), the Bayesian-evidence solver can estimate an overall factor (Regularization and choice of the regularization strength, “Unknown error scale”).

Alternative fit statistics

fit_statistics() reports, for the measured and fitted total intensities \(I_i\), \(F_i\) (background included, so that both are positive):

\[\chi^2_r = \frac1M\sum_i\frac{(I_i-F_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2},\qquad \frac{\chi^2_P}{M} = \frac1M\sum_i\frac{(I_i-F_i)^2}{F_i},\]
\[\frac{G}{M} = \frac2M\sum_i\left[I_i\ln\frac{I_i}{F_i}-(I_i-F_i)\right],\qquad D_{KL} = \sum_i p_i\ln\frac{p_i}{\hat p_i},\quad p_i=\frac{I_i}{\sum_jI_j},\ \hat p_i=\frac{F_i}{\sum_jF_j} .\]

  • Pearson’s \(\chi^2_P\) and the Poisson deviance (G-test) \(G\) are the \(\chi^2\) and the likelihood-ratio statistic for Poisson noise, \(\sigma_i^2=F_i\). Both are \(\approx M\) for a correct model only if \(I\) is in counts. For intensities in other units they scale with the unit (\(I\to cI\) multiplies both by \(c\)), so they cannot replace \(\chi^2_r=1\) as a target.

  • \(D_{KL}\) of the normalized curves is scale-free (SASfit’s G-test is \(2D_{KL}\)) and needs no errors, but it has no target value: it can monitor or compare fits, not choose a regularization parameter.

  • Without \(\sigma_i\) a stopping target therefore needs a noise model (above), or a parameter choice that does not use the absolute error level: GCV, the L-curve, or the Bayesian evidence with an estimated error scale.

All four statistics are shown in the GUI’s result panel. The EM solvers also store SASfit’s G-test in diagnostics["g_test_final"].
